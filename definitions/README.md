# Tax Definitions

Tax terms, forms and schedules in plain English. These definitions don't change from year to year; the dollar amounts and limits for a given year are on that year's [Form 1040 map](../form-1040/).

Browse them as a searchable page: [definitions/index.html](index.html).

## Filing basics

**Considered unmarried**  
A married person who can file as head of household because they file separately, paid more than half the cost of the home, their spouse didn't live there during the last six months of the year, and a qualifying child lived with them more than half the year.

**Filing status**  
The category you file under: single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household, or qualifying surviving spouse. It sets your standard deduction, tax brackets and which credits you can claim. It's based on your situation on the last day of the year.

**Head of household (HOH)**  
A status for unmarried people (or married people treated as unmarried) who pay more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person. It has a bigger standard deduction and lower tax than single.

**Married filing jointly (MFJ)**  
One return for a married couple, combining both spouses' income and deductions. Usually gives the lowest tax. Both spouses sign and are each responsible for the whole tax.

**Married filing separately (MFS)**  
Each spouse files their own return. Often costs more and blocks several credits, but can make sense when one spouse won't file jointly or wants to be responsible only for their own tax.

**Qualifying surviving spouse (QSS)**  
A status for the two years after a spouse dies, if the survivor hasn't remarried and has a dependent child living with them. It uses the same tax rates and standard deduction as married filing jointly. In the year of death itself, the survivor can usually file jointly.

**Single**  
Filing status for someone unmarried (or legally divorced or separated) on the last day of the year who doesn't qualify for a better status.

**Tax year**  
The calendar year the return covers. A 2025 return reports what happened from January 1 to December 31, 2025, and is filed in early 2026.

## Income

**Alimony**  
Payments to a former spouse under a divorce or separation agreement. Under agreements signed before 2019 it's taxable to the person who receives it and deductible by the person who pays it. Under later agreements it's neither.

**Capital gain or loss**  
The profit or loss when you sell an investment or other property. Gain on property held more than a year is long-term and taxed at lower rates.

**Child support**  
Payments to support a child after a divorce or separation. Never taxable to the person who receives it and never deductible by the person who pays it.

**Dividends**  
Money a company or fund pays to its shareholders. Ordinary dividends are taxed like wages; qualified dividends get lower capital gains rates.

**Earned income**  
Money you get for working: wages, salaries, tips, and net earnings from self-employment. It matters for the earned income credit, the child tax credit and IRA contributions.

**Gambling winnings**  
Money won from lotteries, casinos, racing and similar. All of it is taxable, even without a W-2G. Losses are deductible only if you itemize, and only up to your winnings.

**Gross income**  
All income you receive that isn't specifically exempt from tax, before any adjustments or deductions.

**Interest**  
What a bank, credit union or bond pays you for using your money. Most interest is taxable; interest from most state and local bonds is tax-exempt.

**Nontaxable income**  
Money you receive that isn't taxed, such as gifts, child support, most veterans' benefits, workers' compensation, and damages for a physical injury.

**Qualified dividends**  
Dividends that meet holding-period and other rules and are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates.

**Self-employment income**  
Profit from work you do for yourself rather than as an employee, usually reported on Schedule C. It's subject to self-employment tax as well as income tax.

**Social Security benefits**  
Retirement, survivor or disability payments from Social Security. Depending on your other income, from none to 85% of them is taxable.

**Taxable income**  
The amount the tax is actually figured on: adjusted gross income minus your standard or itemized deduction and certain other deductions.

**Tips**  
Money customers give for service. Tips are taxable and must be reported to your employer. Some tips qualify for a separate deduction starting in 2025.

**Total income**  
The sum of every income line on Form 1040: wages, interest, dividends, retirement income, capital gains and other income from Schedule 1.

**Unearned income**  
Income that doesn't come from working, such as interest, dividends, capital gains, rents, unemployment and Social Security.

**Unemployment compensation**  
Benefits paid while you're out of work. It's taxable and counts as unearned income.

**Wages**  
Pay from an employer, reported in Box 1 of Form W-2.

## Adjustments & deductions

**Adjusted gross income (AGI)**  
Total income minus adjustments to income. Many limits, credits and phaseouts are based on it.

**Adjustments to income**  
Specific expenses subtracted from total income to get AGI, such as student loan interest, educator expenses, IRA contributions and half of self-employment tax. You get them whether or not you itemize. Also called 'above-the-line' deductions.

**Charitable contribution**  
A gift of money or property to a qualified charity. Deductible only if you itemize, and only the part that's more than the value of anything you got back.

**Deduction**  
An amount subtracted from income before tax is figured. A deduction lowers taxable income, so it saves you your tax rate times the deduction.

**Educator expenses**  
An adjustment for K–12 teachers and aides who work at least 900 hours a school year, for classroom supplies they paid for themselves.

**Fair market value (FMV)**  
What a willing buyer would pay a willing seller. Donated property is usually deducted at its fair market value, not at what you paid.

**Itemized deductions**  
Specific expenses listed on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction: medical costs above a percentage of AGI, state and local taxes, mortgage interest, charitable gifts and a few others. Worth it only when the total is bigger than the standard deduction.

**Modified adjusted gross income (MAGI)**  
AGI with certain items added back. The add-backs differ for each credit or deduction, so check the rule you're working with. For many people MAGI equals AGI.

**Mortgage interest deduction**  
An itemized deduction for interest on a loan used to buy, build or improve a main or second home, limited to interest on a set amount of debt.

**Points**  
Up-front fees paid to a lender to get a lower interest rate. On a home purchase they're often deductible right away; on a refinance they're deducted over the life of the loan.

**Qualified business income (QBI) deduction**  
A deduction of up to 20% of profit from a sole proprietorship, rental or pass-through business. Figured on Form 8995 or 8995-A.

**SALT deduction**  
The itemized deduction for state and local taxes: income or sales tax, real estate tax and personal property tax. It has an annual cap.

**Senior deduction**  
A deduction for taxpayers 65 or older, new for 2025 through 2028, claimed on Schedule 1-A whether or not you itemize. It shrinks at higher incomes.

**Standard deduction**  
A fixed amount, based on filing status, age and blindness, that you can subtract instead of listing actual expenses. Most people take it.

**Student loan interest deduction**  
An adjustment for interest paid on a loan used for higher education. Limited each year and phased out at higher incomes.

## Tax & payments

**Balance due**  
What you still owe when your total tax is more than your payments and refundable credits.

**Estimated tax payments**  
Quarterly payments made with Form 1040-ES on income that has no withholding, such as self-employment or investment income.

**Kiddie tax**  
A rule that taxes a child's investment income above a set amount at the parents' tax rate, figured on Form 8615.

**Refund**  
Money the IRS sends back when your payments and refundable credits are more than your total tax.

**Self-employment tax**  
Social Security and Medicare tax for people who work for themselves. It's both the employee and employer share, figured on Schedule SE. Half of it is an adjustment to income.

**Tax**  
The amount you owe on your taxable income, figured from the Tax Table or tax rate schedules.

**Tax bracket**  
A range of income taxed at one rate. Only the income inside each range is taxed at that range's rate, so moving into a higher bracket never makes your total tax jump on all your income.

**Tax Table**  
The chart in the Form 1040 instructions that gives the tax for each $50 band of taxable income. Used when taxable income is under $100,000.

**Withholding**  
Income tax an employer or payer takes out of your pay and sends to the IRS for you. It's a prepayment that counts toward your total tax.

## Credits

**Additional child tax credit (ACTC)**  
The refundable part of the child tax credit, for families whose tax is too low to use the whole credit. Based on earned income.

**Advance premium tax credit (APTC)**  
Premium tax credit paid directly to your insurer during the year to lower your monthly premium. If you received too much, you may have to pay some back.

**American opportunity tax credit (AOTC)**  
A credit for the first four years of college for a student enrolled at least half-time. Part of it is refundable. Figured on Form 8863.

**Child and dependent care credit**  
A nonrefundable credit for part of what you pay for care of a child under 13 (or a disabled spouse or dependent) so you can work or look for work. Figured on Form 2441.

**Child tax credit (CTC)**  
A credit for each qualifying child under 17 with a valid Social Security number. Figured on Schedule 8812.

**Credit**  
An amount subtracted directly from your tax, dollar for dollar. A $1,000 credit saves $1,000, which makes credits worth more than deductions of the same size.

**Credit for other dependents (ODC)**  
A smaller nonrefundable credit for dependents who don't qualify for the child tax credit, such as older children or a parent you support.

**Credit for the elderly or disabled**  
A nonrefundable credit for people 65 or older, or retired on permanent and total disability, with low income. Figured on Schedule R.

**Earned income credit (EIC / EITC)**  
A refundable credit for workers with low to moderate income. It's larger with qualifying children, and there are limits on income, investment income and age.

**Energy efficient home improvement credit**  
A credit for insulation, efficient windows and doors, heat pumps and energy audits. Ended for property placed in service after 2025.

**Foreign tax credit**  
A credit for income tax paid to another country, so the same income isn't fully taxed twice. Usually figured on Form 1116.

**Lifetime learning credit (LLC)**  
A nonrefundable education credit for any college or job-skills courses, with no limit on the number of years. Figured on Form 8863.

**Nonrefundable credit**  
A credit that can lower your tax to zero but not below. Any extra is lost (or carried forward, for some credits).

**Phaseout**  
An income range over which a credit or deduction gradually shrinks to zero. Below the range you get the full amount; above it, none.

**Premium tax credit (PTC)**  
A refundable credit that helps pay for health insurance bought through the Marketplace. Advance payments made during the year are reconciled on Form 8962.

**Refundable credit**  
A credit that's paid to you even if it's more than your tax, so it can create or increase a refund.

**Residential clean energy credit**  
A credit for installing solar, wind, geothermal or battery storage systems in your home. Ended for systems installed after 2025.

**Saver's credit**  
A nonrefundable credit for low and moderate income people who contribute to a retirement plan or IRA. Figured on Form 8880.

## Family & dependents

**Age test**  
For a qualifying child: under 19 at the end of the year, under 24 if a full-time student, or any age if permanently and totally disabled. The child also has to be younger than you, unless disabled.

**Custodial parent**  
The parent the child lived with for more nights during the year. Keeps head of household, the earned income credit and the dependent care credit even if they release the dependency to the other parent.

**Dependent**  
A qualifying child or qualifying relative you can claim on your return. Dependents can make you eligible for credits and filing statuses.

**Joint return test**  
A dependent generally can't file a joint return, unless it's only to claim a refund of withheld tax.

**Noncustodial parent**  
The parent the child lived with fewer nights. Can claim the child for the dependency and child tax credit only if the custodial parent signs Form 8332.

**Qualifying child**  
A dependent who meets the relationship, age, residency, support and joint return tests: generally your child, stepchild, foster child, sibling or a descendant of one, who lived with you more than half the year.

**Qualifying person**  
The person who makes you eligible for head of household or another benefit. Who counts is different for each benefit, so check the specific rule.

**Qualifying relative**  
A dependent who isn't a qualifying child, such as a parent or another relative you support. Their gross income must be under a set amount and you must provide more than half their support. Some non-relatives qualify if they lived with you all year.

**Relationship test**  
The rule about how a dependent must be related to you.

**Residency test**  
For a qualifying child: lived with you for more than half the year. Temporary absences, such as school or a hospital stay, count as time lived with you.

**Support test**  
For a qualifying child: the child didn't pay more than half of their own support. For a qualifying relative: you paid more than half of their support.

**Tax family**  
Everyone on the return: you, your spouse if filing jointly, and your dependents.

**Tiebreaker rules**  
Rules for deciding who claims a child when more than one person could. A parent wins over a non-parent; between parents, the one the child lived with more nights; if still tied, the higher AGI.

## Retirement

**401(k)**  
A retirement plan through your employer. Contributions come out of your paycheck before tax (or after tax, for a Roth 401(k)), and many employers match part of them.

**Active participant**  
Someone covered by a workplace retirement plan during the year, shown by Box 13 on Form W-2. It can limit how much of a traditional IRA contribution is deductible.

**Annuity**  
A series of regular payments, usually for life or for a set number of years.

**Basis**  
Money already taxed that you put into an investment or retirement account. It comes back to you tax-free. For IRAs, basis is tracked on Form 8606.

**Catch-up contribution**  
An extra amount people 50 and older can put into an IRA or workplace plan on top of the regular yearly limit.

**Compensation (for IRAs)**  
Pay from work that you can base an IRA contribution on: wages, tips, self-employment earnings, and alimony under pre-2019 agreements. Interest, dividends and pensions don't count.

**Early distribution**  
A withdrawal from a retirement account before age 59½. Usually subject to a 10% additional tax on top of regular income tax, unless an exception applies.

**Pension**  
Regular payments from an employer's retirement plan after you retire.

**Qualified charitable distribution (QCD)**  
A gift sent straight from your IRA to a charity once you're 70½ or older. It isn't counted as income and can count toward your required minimum distribution, but it can't also be deducted as a charitable gift.

**Required minimum distribution (RMD)**  
The minimum amount you must withdraw each year from most retirement accounts once you reach the required age.

**Rollover**  
Moving money from one retirement account to another. Done correctly (generally within 60 days, or directly between custodians), it isn't taxed.

**Roth IRA**  
An IRA funded with after-tax money. Contributions aren't deductible, but qualified withdrawals, including earnings, are tax-free.

**Simplified Method**  
The IRS worksheet for working out the tax-free part of each pension or annuity payment when you paid into the plan with after-tax money. Uses a set number of expected payments based on your age when payments start.

**Spousal IRA**  
An IRA contribution for a spouse with little or no pay, based on the working spouse's compensation, on a joint return.

**Traditional IRA**  
An individual retirement account you open yourself. Contributions may be deductible; withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

## Preparer rules

**Circular 230**  
The Treasury regulations that govern practice before the IRS, including the duty to exercise due diligence in preparing returns.

**Due diligence**  
A paid preparer's duty to make reasonable inquiries, ask more questions when information seems incorrect, inconsistent or incomplete, and document the answers.

**Form 8867**  
The paid preparer's due diligence checklist. Required for returns claiming the earned income credit, child tax credit, credit for other dependents, American opportunity credit, or head of household status.

**Knowledge requirement**  
The rule that a preparer can't ignore what a reasonable preparer would know, and must ask more questions when something doesn't add up.

**PTIN**  
Preparer Tax Identification Number. Anyone paid to prepare or help prepare federal returns must have one, renew it every year, and enter it on the returns they prepare.

## Forms & schedules

**Form 1040**  
The main federal income tax return for individuals. Almost every other individual form or schedule feeds a number onto one of its lines.

**Form 1040-SR**  
A version of Form 1040 for people 65 and older. Same lines, larger print, and a standard deduction chart printed on the form.

**Form 1095-A**  
The Health Insurance Marketplace statement. If you get one, you need Form 8962 to reconcile the premium tax credit.

**Form 1098**  
Reports mortgage interest and points you paid to a lender.

**Form 1098-E**  
Reports student loan interest you paid.

**Form 1098-T**  
Tuition statement from a college, used for education credits.

**Form 1099-DIV**  
Reports dividends and capital gain distributions from stocks and mutual funds.

**Form 1099-G**  
Reports certain government payments, such as unemployment compensation and state tax refunds.

**Form 1099-INT**  
Reports interest paid to you by a bank, credit union or other payer.

**Form 1099-NEC**  
Reports pay to someone who isn't an employee, such as a freelancer or independent contractor.

**Form 1099-R**  
Reports distributions from pensions, annuities, IRAs and other retirement plans. The code in Box 7 tells what kind of distribution it was.

**Form 2106**  
Employee Business Expenses, now used only by Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis government officials, and employees with impairment-related work expenses.

**Form 2441**  
Child and Dependent Care Expenses: figures the dependent care credit and reports employer-provided dependent care benefits.

**Form 5329**  
Additional taxes on retirement plans, including the 10% early distribution tax and its exceptions.

**Form 5695**  
Residential Energy Credits: figures the clean energy and home improvement credits.

**Form 8332**  
The custodial parent's release of a child's dependency claim to the noncustodial parent.

**Form 8606**  
Nondeductible IRAs: tracks after-tax money in traditional IRAs and reports Roth conversions.

**Form 8863**  
Education Credits: figures the American opportunity and lifetime learning credits.

**Form 8880**  
Figures the saver's credit.

**Form 8962**  
Premium Tax Credit: reconciles advance payments of the credit with the amount you qualify for.

**Form SSA-1099**  
The Social Security Administration's yearly statement of benefits paid to you.

**Form W-2**  
Wage and Tax Statement. Your employer's report of your pay and the taxes withheld.

**Form W-2G**  
Reports certain gambling winnings and any tax withheld from them.

**Information return**  
A form a bank, employer or other payer sends to both you and the IRS reporting money paid to you, such as a W-2 or 1099. The IRS matches these against your return.

**Schedule**  
An extra page attached to Form 1040 for details that don't fit on the main form, such as business income (Schedule C) or itemized deductions (Schedule A).

**Schedule 1**  
Additional income that doesn't have its own line on Form 1040, and adjustments to income.

**Schedule 1-A**  
New for 2025. Figures the deductions for qualified tips, qualified overtime, car loan interest and the senior deduction.

**Schedule 2**  
Additional taxes, such as self-employment tax, the 10% additional tax on early retirement withdrawals, and repayment of excess advance premium tax credit.

**Schedule 3**  
Additional nonrefundable credits (Part I) and other payments and refundable credits (Part II).

**Schedule 8812**  
Figures the child tax credit, the credit for other dependents and the additional child tax credit.

**Schedule A**  
Itemized deductions.

**Schedule B**  
Interest and ordinary dividends, when they're over the reporting threshold, plus questions about foreign accounts.

**Schedule C**  
Profit or loss from a business you run as a sole proprietor.

**Schedule D**  
Capital gains and losses, summarized from Form 8949.

**Schedule E**  
Income from rentals, royalties, partnerships, S corporations, estates and trusts.

**Schedule EIC**  
Lists each qualifying child for the earned income credit.

**Schedule SE**  
Figures self-employment tax.
